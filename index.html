Part III: Blog Post

I built a tiny automated composer: it learns an n-th order Markov chain from a monophonic melody, samples a continuation, and schedules the result through Web Audio oscillators. No ML model, no Markov library, just transition counts and weighted random choice.

I chose Markov chain learning over cellular automata or pitch-set operations because the training/generation split maps cleanly onto how we talk about musical style in class. You feed it a known piece; it extracts 'what pitch tends to follow this context?' and turns that statistical sketch back into sound.

The chain stores counts in a map keyed by the last n pitches. At generation time, each new pitch is sampled from the row for that context. Temperature reshapes the distribution: at zero you always pick the most common successor; above one you flatten probabilities and get wilder lines. When a context was never seen in training, the implementation walks down to lower-order rows and finally a global pitch histogram, which is a practical detail lectures often skip.

For playback I reused the Lab 3 pattern: triangle oscillators, short exponential attack/release on a per-note GainNode, and scheduling against audioContext.currentTime so notes do not drift. The piano roll canvas compares training (blue) and generated (orange) sequences so you can literally see when the chain repeats Twinkle fragments versus when it wanders.

One of he insights I had for this assignment was how Markov Chains could be an excellent use case for lofi/abstract games where one would want original music everytime.
